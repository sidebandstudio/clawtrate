# A Clawtrate account: adding a device by signing in

How a person links their OpenClaw gateway to a Clawtrate account once, on the gateway's machine, and then adds any other computer or phone by signing in to that account, with no pairing code, no SSH and no command (issue #638). It joins the remote case of #629, the phone of #14 and #286, and the relay of #254. This is a design for sign-off: nothing hosted is built until it is signed off.

**Established** marks what the repository or OpenClaw's own source and documentation show today. **Proposed** marks this design's choice. **Not established** marks what could not be shown here, with what would show it. OpenClaw facts are from the published packages `openclaw@2026.10.6` (the version Clawtrate is tested against) and `openclaw@2026.11.10`, read only; `<openclaw>/docs/…` and `<openclaw>/dist/…` are paths inside either package, and line numbers in `dist/` are 2026.10.6's (chunk names carry a build hash; 2026.11.10 has the same rules in its own chunks).

This version takes in an independent security review. Its findings are in the text where they apply; "What changed since the first draft" below lists them.

## The page to decide from

### What an account is for

An account lets a person's devices find their gateway and lets their own devices say who else gets in. A person links their gateway to the account once, with one click on the gateway's machine. After that, a new computer or phone signs in with GitHub or Google, the person confirms it on a device they already have by picking the words the new device shows, and it is in: no address, no token, no code, no Terminal. Removing a device from any of their devices takes it out of the gateway at once. Someone who never makes an account keeps everything that works today.

### Linking a gateway and adding a device, step by step

The case this is for: OpenClaw runs on a Windows PC, and the person wants to use it from a MacBook.

**On the gateway's machine, once** (the Windows PC):

1. Install Clawtrate and open it. "Where does OpenClaw run?" → **This computer** → **Connect**. Clawtrate connects with full control and no approval, because OpenClaw lets a program on its own computer pair without asking (the same-computer flow of #629; on Windows it needs the Windows half of §12). Where the gateway's owner has turned that off (`autoApproveLocal: false`), it asks once for the approval #629 describes. _1 click._
2. Clawtrate offers "Use this gateway from your other devices". **Link with GitHub** or **Link with Google**. _1 click._ Behind it, Clawtrate starts its connector and lets it into the gateway itself; nothing is shown or asked.
3. The browser opens on the account page. **Continue with Google** (or GitHub), and the provider's own consent screen the first time. _1 or 2 clicks._ If the browser isn't signed in to Google or GitHub yet, the person signs in there as they always do; that is typed into the provider, not into Clawtrate.
4. Back in Clawtrate: "Linked to your account. Sign in on your other devices." _0 clicks._

**On each other device, after** (the MacBook):

5. Install Clawtrate and open it. **Sign in**. _1 click._
6. The browser: **Continue with Google**. _1 click._
7. The Mac shows three words, for example "otter · maple · drum", and "Confirm on a device you already have".
8. On the Windows PC (or any device already in), Clawtrate shows "A device that says it is a MacBook Pro wants to use this gateway. Only pick if it is in front of you now." First come **I didn't start this** and **None of these match**, then five sets of words. The person picks the set the Mac shows. _1 click._ Picking is what checks that the two screens are talking to each other and to nobody in between.
9. The Mac says "Confirmed on Windows PC" and connects, and the person's bots, tasks, conversations and pinned bots are there. _0 clicks._ Until the relay ships, this needs the two on the same network or the same private network (§8.2).

**Count.** Gateway's machine: 3 to 4 clicks once, then 1 click for each device added. Each new device: 2 clicks. **Typed into Clawtrate: nothing. Terminal: never.** Today the same case needs SSH or commands on the Windows PC, and approvals that are each a command typed there (#629).

A gateway on a server with no screen is the one exception: installing the connector there is one command, once (§8.4), because there is no app to click.

### What the account never holds

The account never holds conversations, gateway tokens, provider credentials, the content of any file, or the gateway's private address. It holds who is signed in, which gateway is linked, and the public keys of the devices on it. It sees the public internet address of each network the person's devices and gateway use, as any service they connect to does (§5).

**Why the service can't add a device by itself:** the program on the gateway's machine lets in only a device that one of the person's own devices has signed for, with a key used for nothing else, and those keys never leave the devices, so the account service, or anyone who takes it over, has nothing to sign with (§4).

### The decisions, with a recommendation and what each costs

1. **Where it runs, for $0.** _Recommended:_ the first version runs only the account service, on Cloudflare's free plan (Workers, with its D1 database and Durable Objects for the waiting room of a join), and carries no conversation through anything the project pays for: devices reach the gateway over the person's own network, their own private network (Tailscale and the like) or SSH. The free plan's 100,000 requests a day fit roughly **10,000 devices in daily use**. Past that, Cloudflare's paid plan is $5 a month and fits roughly 30,000; a million devices cost on the order of $100 to $150 a month. What this accepts: the service stops for the rest of the day (until 00:00 UTC) if the free limits run out, it depends on a provider's free plan that can change, and every connection still works while it is down. The relay that reaches a gateway from anywhere comes after the cryptographic review; on the same free plan it fits only about **75 devices in daily use**, about $90 a month at 10,000 and $8,000 to $9,000 a month at a million (§9). The code is open source and the same service runs on any small machine for someone who hosts it themselves.
2. **Sign-in.** _Decided:_ GitHub and Google first. The account service is the only program registered with each provider; the apps never hold a provider's secret or token, and the service reads the provider's answer once for the person's id and keeps no provider token (§7). _A third, signing in with a ChatGPT account:_ OpenAI's [Sign in with ChatGPT](https://developers.openai.com/siwc/quickstart) exists, but its identity sign-in is "currently available to selected commercial partners through a limited trial" (read 2026-11-25), so it needs OpenAI to admit Clawtrate first; adding it then is one provider module, not a redesign.
3. **Confirming a new device.** _Recommended:_ pick the matching words on a device already in (one click when it is right). It is what keeps the service from admitting anyone. A sign-in alone admitting a device would mean whoever controls the service, or the person's Google or GitHub session, could get into their gateway; that option is laid out in §4.7 and not recommended. A passkey can later drop the click on devices that support it.
4. **"The same on every device."** _Recommended for the first version:_ conversations, tasks and routines (already on the gateway), the rail's pins, sections and hidden bots (already there), read and unread, the theme and the gateway's name follow the person, kept on their own gateway. Switches that hide what bots do (Show commands, Show thinking, other tools' sessions, the gateway's own bot) follow only when turned on, never off, because anything with write access to the gateway can change that store. The run limit, "New bots too", drafts and the window's layout stay on each device (§6).
5. **One gateway per account in the first version.** Linking a second needs one of the person's devices to sign for it. This keeps someone with a stolen sign-in from linking their own gateway and catching the person's next device (§10).

### What waits for the relay's cryptographic review

**Ships before it:** the account service, signing in, linking a gateway, the device list, confirming and removing devices, the gateway letting a confirmed device in with no approval typed, the settings that follow a person, and reaching the gateway over the same network, a private network or SSH. None of these carries a conversation through the relay. The account's own signing protocol is new and gets its own independent review before anything built on it merges (§11).

**Waits for it:** reaching a gateway from anywhere through Clawtrate's relay (a phone on cellular, a gateway in a virtual machine behind its host's network, a laptop away from home), because that is what the review is of. `docs/decisions.md` says nothing builds on the relay protocol until the review has passed, and this design keeps that: nothing in the account imports `packages/relay-protocol`.

### What changed since the first draft

- **Each new device gets a one-use pass to the gateway**, made by the connector and handed only to that device over its own connection, because the gateway refuses a device that brings no credential at all. Nothing changes on screen (§4.4).
- **The connector lets itself into the gateway with a pass the app makes**, so it never needs the gateway's shared token, for the same reason (§4.3).
- **Signing to the account service and signing for a new device use separate keys and labels**, so the service can never get a device to sign "let this one in" by asking it to sign something else (§4.2).
- **The connector writes every request it passes to the gateway itself**, so a device can never pass as a program on the gateway's own computer and skip the checks (§11).
- **The confirmation shows five sets of words, after "I didn't start this" and "None of these match"**, and the gateway's own machine, not the service, enforces the pause after a refusal. It stays one click when it is right (§4.4).
- **An account has one gateway in the first version**, so someone with a stolen sign-in can't put their own gateway in front of the person (§10).
- **Removing a stolen device also offers to remove everything it added**, and the account page can't remove the gateway's own computer or its connector (§4.6).
- **Only what can't hide anything follows a person through the gateway** (§6).
- **What the service sees is stated exactly:** public addresses, the provider's answer for a moment, and its platform's logs (§5).

## 1. The promise, in a person's words

These are the sentences the screens and the website may say. Each rests on a section below.

- **Linking.** "Link this gateway to your Clawtrate account. You do this once, on the computer OpenClaw runs on." One click there, then the provider's sign-in. An account has one gateway. Linking changes nothing in how the gateway works for anything already connected to it.
- **Adding a device.** "Sign in on your other computer or phone, then confirm it on a device you already have." The new device shows three words; the person picks them on the other device. From then on the new device reconnects by itself, with nothing to sign in to again until it is removed.
- **Removing a device.** "Remove it from Settings on any of your devices, or from your account page in a browser if you no longer have any." It loses access to the gateway as soon as the connector hears of it: at once from a device that is connected to the gateway, or, from the account page, when the connector is next online, with the page showing the removal as waiting until the connector confirms it. Its saved credentials then stop working. Clawtrate offers to remove what it added too.
- **Unlinking.** "Unlink on the gateway's computer, or from any of your devices." The devices the account added are removed from the gateway too, unless the person keeps them (§8.5).
- **Without an account.** "You don't need an account to use Clawtrate." Connecting on the same computer, a typed address, SSH and setup codes all work as they do today, and an account is never asked for.
- **When the service is down.** "Your devices keep working with your gateway." Only signing in a new device and the account page need the service.

## 3. What OpenClaw offers, established first

The account can only do what OpenClaw lets a program on the gateway's machine do. `docs/design/setup-effortless.md` (§2, §3 and §5) established much of this live on OpenClaw 2026.10.6; this section keeps what this design rests on and adds what it found.

**Every connection must bring a credential before pairing is considered** (established, both versions). In the gateway's token or password mode, a hello that carries none of the shared secret, a bootstrap token or a device token is refused and its socket closed before any pairing request is filed (`<openclaw>/dist/message-handler-*.mjs` lines 1263 to 1269; `auth-*.mjs` lines 280 to 291; `node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 70 to 160, where only a bootstrap or device token can make the hello authorized without the shared secret). A device's signature covers the token it presents (`node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 211 to 219), so nothing in between can add one to a device's hello. So a new device can't just "ask" the gateway: it needs a credential of its own first.

**A bootstrap token is that credential, and it binds to the first device that uses it** (established, both versions). `device.pair.setupCode` (needs `operator.admin`) issues a setup code whose payload is base64url JSON holding the address and a bootstrap token (`<openclaw>/dist/setup-code-*.mjs` lines 188 and 230 to 300). The token lasts 10 minutes (`DEVICE_BOOTSTRAP_TOKEN_TTL_MS`, `device-pairing-identity-*.mjs` line 24). On first use it is bound to that device's id and public key, and a different key presenting it is refused (`device-bootstrap.worker-kernel-*.mjs` lines 229 to 290). It allows only the scopes of its profile: the default mobile profile allows `operator.admin`, `operator.read`, `operator.write`, `operator.approvals`, `operator.questions` and `operator.talk.secrets`, and falls back to a limited profile without admin when the address isn't `wss://` or loopback; no profile a setup code issues allows `operator.pairing` (`device-bootstrap-profile-*.mjs`; `setup-code-*.mjs` lines 54 to 63). A gateway bound to loopback makes a code only when the caller gives an address (`publicUrl`); a loopback `ws://` address is accepted (`setup-code-*.mjs` lines 64 to 75 and 186). For a client other than OpenClaw's own, the request stays pending until someone approves it (`<openclaw>/docs/cli/qr.md` line 27, both versions). And #286's research (its §2, on that branch) found that whoever presents a code first can pose as one of OpenClaw's own apps and be let in by the code alone, up to the code's profile. In this design only the device the code was made for ever holds it (§4.4), so that is acceptable, and stated.

**A program on the gateway's own computer can pair without approval** (established, both versions). A first pairing over loopback with no forwarded headers and no browser `Origin` is approved silently with whatever its credential allows, unless `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveLocal` is `false` (default true): `shouldAllowSilentLocalPairing` in `node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 178 to 185, used at `message-handler-*.mjs` lines 1894 to 1902; `<openclaw>/docs/gateway/pairing.md`, "Silent local pairing" (2026.10.6 line 229, 2026.11.10 line 291). A later scope upgrade is silent only for a hello that uses the shared secret or no auth; one on a device token waits for approval (the same function), and a reconnect that asks for broader scopes "creates a new pending upgrade request" (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/operator-scopes.md`, 2026.10.6 line 557, 2026.11.10 line 892).

**A paired device can approve another device** (established, both versions; `<openclaw>/docs/gateway/operator-scopes.md`, 2026.10.6 lines 531 and 597 to 610, 2026.11.10 lines 866 and 922 to 945; `dist/devices-*.mjs` lines 355 to 366). `device.pair.approve` needs `operator.pairing`, and for each scope the request asks for, that scope or `operator.admin`; a request for `operator.admin` needs admin. A device whose token lacks admin can manage only its own entry. So the connector must hold `operator.pairing` and `operator.admin`. Approval issues a token for every role the request named (`device-pairing-core.worker-*.mjs` line 117).

**A pending request is bound to the device's key, and a replayed hello files nothing** (established, both versions). The gateway derives the device id from the public key (`message-handler-*.mjs` lines 952 to 956) and checks the hello's signature over this connection's nonce within a bounded clock skew (line 958 on). The pending record keeps the public key, roles, scopes and remote address (`device-pairing-identity-*.mjs` lines 241 to 283) and expires after 5 minutes (line 602). Approval goes by request id. So the connector doesn't need to know which connection a request came from: matching the request's public key to the device list is enough (§4.5).

**How the gateway decides a connection is local** (established, both versions). A loopback socket with no `Forwarded`, `X-Forwarded-*`, `X-Real-IP` or Tailscale headers is "direct-local"; one with them is trusted only when its socket address is in `gateway.trustedProxies`, and then its client address comes from the headers; a chain that resolves to loopback is refused; anything else with those headers is refused as unattributable (`ingress-attribution-*.mjs` lines 95 to 118; `net-*.mjs` lines 37 to 47). This is decided per HTTP request, not per socket (lines 119 to 124). And once loopback is a trusted proxy, any local process can claim any client address; the gateway then uses that address for its sign-in rate limit and for `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveCidrs` (node pairing only; `message-handler-*.mjs` lines 1903 to 1914). §11 says what the connector does about both. Changing proxy trust applies without a restart (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/config-gateway.md` 2026.10.6 line 157; live in #286's research §5).

**What the gateway listens on** (established). Loopback by default on port 18789; `lan`, `tailnet`, `custom` or `auto` binds need gateway authentication; its own TLS; Tailscale Serve (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/config-gateway.md`, 2026.10.6 line 145, 2026.11.10 line 146). Changing the bind or TLS restarts the gateway under the default reload mode `hybrid` (the same file, 2026.10.6 lines 330 and 359 to 362). **Proposed:** the design changes none of these. The gateway stays on loopback; the connector does the listening (§11).

**What a plugin can do** (established for 2026.11.10, read only). The principal-bound remote ingress a plugin can open is offered "only for bundled plugins and verified official installations" (`<openclaw>/docs/plugins/sdk-gateway-ingress.md`), so a Clawtrate plugin can't be the network entrance. The connector is an ordinary client on loopback instead, which needs nothing installed into the gateway.

**Who a device is to the gateway's per-person settings** (established, both versions). Every operator connection without a verified user gets the gateway's owner profile while `gateway.roles` is unset (`gateway-owner-profile-*.mjs` lines 46 and 47), so two of a person's devices paired by device token are the same user for `users.prefs`. With `gateway.roles` set, a device-token connection gets `no_durable_identity` and nothing follows it (`users-*.mjs` lines 338 to 347). `users.prefs.set` needs only `operator.write` (`core-method-policy-*.mjs` lines 2434 to 2438), and its handler also admits a bot's runtime during a turn with a single participant (`users-*.mjs` lines 279 to 292).

**What OpenClaw itself has for remote access** (established, 2026.11.10 only). An experimental, off-by-default Lab, "OpenClaw ID remote access" (`<openclaw>/docs/concepts/experimental-features.md`; `<openclaw>/docs/cli/id.md`), keeps owner devices certified by a root key derived from a recovery kit, and a discovery service that "can relay a pending request but cannot admit" (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/remote.md` line 74). It is not in 2026.10.6 and needs the Lab turned on and its own sign-in. This design does not depend on it and changes nothing it does.

## 4. How a signed-in device is let in

### 4.1 Who decides

The gateway decides whether a device is paired, as everywhere in Clawtrate. **Proposed:** the party that asks the gateway to approve an account device is the **connector**, on the gateway's machine, using the gateway's own `device.pair.approve` under its own `operator.pairing` and `operator.admin`. It makes a pass for a device and approves its request only when that device is on the gateway's **device list**, and a device is on the list only when one of the person's own devices signed for it after the person picked the matching words. So the decision is the person's twice over: they linked the connector on their machine, and they confirmed each device on another of their own.

The account service introduces devices to each other and keeps a waiting room for a join. It holds no key that can sign for a device, and the connector believes nothing the service says that would let someone in. The rule throughout: **anything that grants access must be signed by one of the person's devices; anything that only takes access away may come from the service too, except for the gateway machine's own device and the connector (§4.6).**

This deliberately differs from "the account vouches" in the wording of #638: an account that vouches by itself is an account whose operator can let anyone in (§4.7).

### 4.2 Keys and what each may sign (proposed)

| Key                           | Held by                                   | Signs or proves, and nothing else                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Session key (Ed25519)         | each device, in its secure storage        | Requests to the account service, in the session context only.                                                                            |
| List key pair (Ed25519)       | each device, in its secure storage        | Entries on a device list (add, remove, restore), removal notices, refusals, and its side of a join transcript.                           |
| TLS client key                | each device                               | The device's client certificate on the connector's direct listener. Ed25519 where the platform allows it, P-256 on iPhone (§14).         |
| Gateway device identity       | each device, as today (`credentials.bin`) | The device's own OpenClaw pairing. Unchanged.                                                                                            |
| Connector list key (Ed25519)  | the connector, in secure storage          | The list's first entry, the gateway's card, passes it hands out. Never reused for the relay's registration later; that gets its own key. |
| Connector TLS key             | the connector                             | Its direct listener's certificate, pinned by every device from the card (§11).                                                           |
| List secret (32 random bytes) | the connector and each device on the list | Sealing the list's entries and the gateway's card, so the service carries them without reading them.                                     |

**Signing contexts.** Every signed object starts with its own fixed label, `clawtrate account <kind> v1` followed by a newline, for each kind: session request, list entry, removal notice, refusal, card, join transcript. A signature checked under one label never verifies under another. A device never signs bytes the service chose: the only service-supplied value it signs is a challenge nonce, inside a session request whose structure the device builds itself. Two keys rather than one with labels are kept on purpose, so a bug in one kind can't reach the other. The account's protocol package imports nothing from `packages/relay-protocol`, which hasn't had its review; it borrows the idea of a label in front of a signed payload, not the code.

### 4.3 Linking, on the gateway's machine

**With Clawtrate on that machine** (it already holds a full pairing from the same-computer flow):

1. The person clicks **Link**.
2. Clawtrate checks the gateway's settings over its admin connection and refuses to link, saying why, while `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveCidrs` is set or the gateway uses trusted-proxy sign-in that allows loopback (§11).
3. Clawtrate asks the gateway for a setup code (`device.pair.setupCode`, with the gateway's own loopback address as `publicUrl`), takes the bootstrap token out of it in memory, and gives only the token to the connector it starts, on the connector's standard input: never in a command line, environment, file or log.
4. The connector, with a new identity, sends its first hello straight to the gateway on loopback, with no forwarded headers, as role `operator` with the scopes the pass allows. OpenClaw pairs it silently as a local first pairing. Where `autoApproveLocal` is `false`, the request waits instead, and Clawtrate approves exactly that request: its request id and the connector's public key, which Clawtrate knows because it started the connector.
5. The connector reconnects on its device token asking for the six. That scope upgrade always waits (§3), and Clawtrate approves exactly it, under the same check. The connector now holds `operator.pairing` and `operator.admin`, and has never seen the gateway's shared token.
6. The connector sets `gateway.trustedProxies` to include exactly `127.0.0.1` and `::1` through the gateway's config call, recording the earlier value and the value it wrote. On unlink it takes out only the entries it added, and only while the setting still holds exactly the value it wrote; if anything else changed it since, it leaves the setting as it is and says so, as #286's design does over a shell.
7. The browser signs in to the account. The account service records the link: this gateway's random id, the connector's public key and the sealed card. If the account already has a gateway, the link is refused unless a device on that gateway's list signs for it (§10).
8. The connector writes the list's first entry, signed by its key and by the linking app's list key: those two are the list's first members, and they are protected (§4.6). This happens on the gateway's machine and involves the service only as a mailbox.

The person sees one click and the provider's sign-in (§8.2). Everything from step 2 to step 8 is automatic.

**On a machine without Clawtrate** (a server): the connector reads the gateway's shared token from OpenClaw's own settings file, as Clawtrate's same-computer connect does (`setup-effortless.md` §3), uses it once for a first hello on loopback asking for the six, which OpenClaw pairs silently, and then forgets it. Where `autoApproveLocal` is `false`, the connector prints the request id and the gateway's approve command once, in the same terminal the person installed it from (§8.4).

Needs a spike on a development gateway before it is built (§14): every step above that names a gateway call.

### 4.4 Adding a device: the join

The new device **N** is signed in. An existing member **A** is any device on the list, including Clawtrate on the gateway's machine. The service carries every message of steps 1 to 5 and can read none of what matters.

1. N makes its keys and asks to join the account's gateway. It first sends only a **commitment**: a hash of its public keys and a random value, so it can't change them later.
2. Each member that is open is told and shows the request. A member that is shown two joins at once refuses both and says so. The first member the person uses answers with its own public key, the list's latest entry, a hash of the gateway's current card (the connector's keys, its TLS pin and its addresses, as signed by the connector) and a random value of its own.
3. N then sends its public keys and its random value. Both sides compute three words from everything exchanged. Because N committed first and A answered before seeing N's keys, someone in the middle (the service, say) can't choose keys that make the words match: each attempt has about one chance in 8.6 billion (three words from a list of 2,048).
4. N shows its words. A shows, first and with the same weight, **I didn't start this** and **None of these match**, then five sets of words: the right one and four A makes with its own randomness, sharing no word with it. Above them: "A device that says it is <name> wants to use this gateway. Only pick if it is in front of you now." The person picks. **The pick is the confirmation.** When someone in the middle has changed the keys, none of the five is what N shows. A person who picks without the new device in front of them is right about one time in six.
5. A right pick: A signs an entry adding N (its list key, TLS client key, gateway device public key, name and platform), seals the list secret and the gateway's card to N, and sends both through the service. The connector checks the entry's signature against the list it holds and appends it. N checks, before it uses the card, that the card's hash is the one A sent in step 2, which the words covered, and that the card is signed by the connector key it names; a card that fails either check ends the join. Then N says "Confirmed on <A's name>".
   A wrong pick, "None of these match" or "I didn't start this": A signs a refusal and sends it to the connector, which then accepts no entry adding a device for an hour and tells every member. "I didn't start this" also tells the person to secure their Google or GitHub account. The pause is enforced by the connector on the gateway's machine, so the service can't lift it.
6. **N gets its pass.** N opens a TLS connection to the connector's direct listener (§11) with its client certificate. The connector checks that N is on the list, has no pairing yet and has no pass outstanding, asks the gateway for a setup code on its admin connection, takes the bootstrap token out in memory, and returns only the token to N on that connection. The token never passes through the service, is written nowhere and lasts at most 10 minutes; the gateway binds it to the first device that uses it, which is N.
7. **N's first pairing.** N opens a second TLS connection and sends a WebSocket upgrade, which the connector rebuilds as its own (§11). N's hello presents the token, as role `operator`, with the scopes the pass allows. The gateway accepts it as a bootstrap sign-in from a remote device and files a pending request. The connector approves it under §4.5's checks. N's next hello, which Clawtrate already retries every few seconds, gets its device token straight from the gateway.
8. **N's full control.** N reconnects on its device token asking for the six. The gateway files a scope upgrade, and the connector approves it under the same checks. N now holds the six, as a device paired the ordinary way does.

Steps 6 to 8 use only calls that exist in both OpenClaw versions, and nothing here changes what the gateway's scopes allow: a device that asked for less gets less. They need the spike of §14 before they are built.

### 4.5 What the connector checks before every approval (proposed, on established facts)

- The pending request's **public key** (not only its device id) is exactly the gateway device key of a current member's entry.
- Its roles are exactly `["operator"]`: approval issues a token for every role named (§3).
- Its scopes are within the six.
- For a first pairing, N had a pass outstanding; for a scope upgrade, N is paired already.
- It **reads the request again immediately before approving**, and approves by its request id with nothing in between.
- It **rejects** every pending request of a device that is not, or is no longer, on the list, and leaves every other pending request for the person, in Clawtrate's Devices pane as today.

### 4.6 Removing a device

From Settings → Devices on any member, or the account page in a browser:

1. A member signs a removal entry. The account page, which has no list key, sends an unsigned removal. The connector accepts an unsigned removal for any member **except** the gateway machine's own device and the connector itself, which only a member's signature or an action on the gateway's machine can remove. Unlinking is the same.
2. The connector removes the device's pairing on the gateway (`device.pair.remove`, then `device.token.revoke` for any token left), rejects its pending requests, closes its connections, drops its keys from the listener, writes a new list secret and seals it to the members that remain. The device's saved credentials stop working at once. A removal from a member connected to the gateway reaches the connector directly; one from the account page waits in the service until the connector, which checks for removals whenever it is online and every minute while it is, takes it, and the page shows it as waiting until then. While the connector is offline, nothing can reach the gateway through it either, so a removed device has no route in through the account in the meantime; a device with its own route (a typed address, SSH) is removed from Settings on a member connected that way.
3. **What it added goes too, by default.** Removing a device offers, ticked, to also remove every entry it signed and every gateway pairing that appeared while it was a member and isn't on the list.
4. **Every unsigned removal is announced** on every member, with **Put it back**. A member signs the restore, and the device pairs again with a new pass (§4.4 steps 6 to 8), without new words.
5. **The connector watches the gateway's own device list both ways.** A device removed elsewhere (Clawtrate's Devices pane, OpenClaw's own page or command) leaves the list too. A paired device that is not on the list and wasn't paired when the gateway was linked is shown on every member as "Paired outside your account", with Remove, so nothing a thief or anyone else added stays hidden.

A device with full control can change the gateway's settings too (its bind, its sign-in, its proxy trust). Removing it ends its access; it doesn't undo what it changed. §10 says what the person checks.

## 5. What the account holds and never holds

**Never holds:** conversations, transcripts, files, tasks, routines, memory, gateway tokens (shared, device or bootstrap), provider credentials or keys, the list secret, the content of the gateway's card (its private addresses, its name, its certificate), any device's private key, any device's name.

**Receives for a moment, never stores:** the sign-in provider's token. The service uses it once to read the person's id, display name and picture address, and then discards it; it is never written or logged. GitHub's tokens for OAuth apps don't expire, so the service revokes each one through GitHub's API right after reading. The Google token is discarded and expires within the hour; no refresh token is asked for.

**Holds** (proposed):

One-time codes and challenges go in request bodies, never in an address, so request logs can't carry them.

**What the service necessarily sees:** who signs in, when, and the public internet address they come from; the public address of the gateway's network, because the connector reaches the service from there and the linking browser signs in from there, and when the connector is online; how many devices each account has; when a device joins or is removed; the sizes and times of what it carries. It does not see the gateway's private address, port or name, or which devices talk to the gateway and when.

**The account page shows** each device's platform, when it was added and the day it was last seen, and not its name: names are sealed with the list secret, which the page doesn't have. Clawtrate's own Settings show the names.

**What a person can delete:** any device (§4.6); the linked gateway (unlinking); a sign-in method, while another remains; the whole account, from the account page, which removes every row at once and asks the connector to remove the devices the account added. Backups held by the hosting provider expire with its restore window, at most 30 days (not established: the exact window of the chosen plan, §14).

**The relay, later,** sees what #286's design lists in its §3: the addresses of both ends, when each connects, sizes and timing, and a random id for the connector. It keeps none of what is said.

## 6. The same on every device

Direction for the first version: a person signs in on any device and finds their gateway the way they left it. Conversations, tasks, routines, bots, memory, skills and files already live on the gateway, so the account's job there is only that every device reaches the same gateway. The rest is the app's own state about the person, surveyed here from the code.

**The rule for what may follow through the gateway.** The store is `users.prefs` on the gateway's owner profile, which anything with `operator.write` can change: a device let in by a setup code, a plugin, possibly a bot during the person's own turn (§3). So only what can't hide anything or widen anything follows the person that way. A switch that hides what bots do follows **only in the direction that shows more**: turning it on on one device turns it on everywhere; turning it off stays on that device. Nothing that needs admin to change, and no limit on a run, is driven from that store.

**Where what follows is kept: on the gateway** (recommended). It is the one store every device already reaches, it is the person's own, and Clawtrate already writes there: `users.prefs` holds up to 4,096 bytes of JSON per key, read with `operator.read` and written with `operator.write` as a compare-and-set, with a `users.prefs.changed` event (`packages/core/src/sidebar.ts`). One new key, `clawtrate.settings`, holds what follows, versioned and bounded like the sidebar's, and each device reads it under the rule above, so a value turned off there is ignored for the "follows when turned on" switches. Two of a person's devices are the same user while `gateway.roles` is unset (§3); with it set, nothing follows, and each device keeps its own, as the rail already does.

The other way, sealed on the account service with the list secret, would make the settings follow a person across gateways and keep them while a gateway is off, and only the person's own devices could write them. It costs a second store and its conflicts. Not recommended for the first version, which has one gateway per account; it is the place for the switches above if they should ever follow in both directions.

**A brand-new device, the moment after sign-in:** once the join ends and the gateway approves it (seconds), it connects and reads, as any connection does now, the bots, the task list, the rail's arrangement and `clawtrate.settings`. It looks like the person's other devices from its first screen, with each conversation loading when opened. What it doesn't have: drafts, the window's layout, a switch turned off elsewhere, the run limit and "New bots too", which start at their defaults.

## 7. Sign-in

### 7.1 The first version: GitHub and Google

**Shape (proposed).** The account service is the only OAuth client registered with each provider: one GitHub OAuth app and one Google web client, with their redirect addresses on the service's own domain and their secrets on the service. A Clawtrate app signs in to the account service, not to the provider: it opens the system browser at the account service with a one-time PKCE challenge (a hash of a secret the app keeps), the service sends the person to the provider and back, and then returns to the app with a one-time code that only the app's secret redeems.

- **Desktop** (macOS, Windows, Linux): the return is `http://127.0.0.1:<port>/callback` on a port the app opened for that sign-in alone; the system browser, never a window inside the app. This works the same on all three systems.
- **iPhone**: `ASWebAuthenticationSession`, which returns to the app that started it.
- **The session** the service then issues is bound to the device's session key: each request is signed by it, in the session context only (§4.2), so a copied session token is useless elsewhere and a session signature can never be taken for a list entry.
- **What is asked of the provider**: only who the person is. GitHub: no scopes (the public profile). Google: `openid` and `profile`. No repository access, no email reading, nothing else. The provider's token is read once and discarded (§5).

| Provider | Registration                                                                                                   | The account's key for the person                           | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GitHub   | One OAuth app (free). Its web flow needs the client secret, so the exchange happens on the service.            | the numeric user id, never the login, which can be renamed | PKCE (S256) and loopback addresses with any port are supported ([GitHub's docs](https://docs.github.com/en/apps/oauth-apps/building-oauth-apps/authorizing-oauth-apps)). Its tokens don't expire, so each is revoked after use. |
| Google   | One OAuth client of the web type (free); the consent screen's brand verified for the logo; no sensitive scopes | the ID token's `sub`, never the email                      | Desktop clients can't keep a secret ([Google's docs](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/native-app)); the web client on the service avoids that.                                                           |

**The same person with both.** Each sign-in is its own account until linked. Settings → Account → "Also sign in with Google" (while signed in with GitHub, or the reverse) adds the second to the same account. Accounts are never joined by a matching email: an email can change hands or be unverified, and joining by it would let someone who controls one sign-in take over the other's account. A person who signs in with the other provider by mistake sees "No gateway on this account" and the other provider's button beside it.

**More providers.** Sign-ins are rows of (provider, the provider's id for the person) pointing at an account. Another OpenID Connect provider is one registration and one small module.

### 7.2 Later: signing in with ChatGPT

OpenAI documents [Sign in with ChatGPT](https://developers.openai.com/siwc/quickstart) (OpenID Connect with PKCE, a client id from OpenAI). Its quickstart says identity sign-in is "currently available to selected commercial partners through a limited trial", and that what is open to open-source projects is using a ChatGPT plan for AI requests (read 2026-11-25). The second isn't the account's business: model access stays on the gateway. So a third sign-in waits until OpenAI lets Clawtrate register a client for identity; it then fits §7.1 unchanged.

### 8.4 A gateway on a server with no screen

One command on the server, which installs the connector from Clawtrate's signed release channel as a service of the person's own account (a user service on Linux) and prints a sign-in address and code; the person opens it on any device and signs in there (the provider's device sign-in shape, RFC 8628, offered by the account service). The page that takes the code says, before anything else, "Continuing links a gateway on a server to your account. Continue only if you ran the install command yourself, just now." The words for each device the person adds show in the connector's output and on that device, and the person confirms on a member instead, which is already signed in. Typed: the one command. This is the only flow with a terminal, because there is nowhere else to click.

### 8.5 Unlinking, and a gateway linked to the wrong account

Settings → Gateway → "Linked to <account>" → **Unlink** (on the gateway's machine or with a member's signature): one click and a confirmation, which lists the devices the account added and offers "Also remove these devices from the gateway" ticked. The connector takes its own entries out of `gateway.trustedProxies` when the setting is still as it wrote it, and otherwise leaves it and says so (§4.3), forgets its keys and removes its own pairing last.

## 9. Hosting and what it costs

### 9.1 Direction and recommendation

Direction: the default path costs nothing to run. A hosted relay is for when the project grows.

**Recommended first version:** the account service on Cloudflare's free plan, in the project's own account; no relay. Devices carry their conversations to the gateway over routes the person already has. The one hosted thing is small: sign-ins, the linked gateway, device rows and a waiting room.

### 9.2 What runs for $0, and what breaks first

Cloudflare's limits as published on 2026-11-25 ([Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/), [Durable Objects pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/)):

| Free plan        | Limit                                                       | Used by                           |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Workers requests | 100,000 a day, 10 ms of CPU each                            | every call to the service         |
| D1 (database)    | 5 million rows read and 100,000 written a day, 5 GB         | accounts, gateways, devices       |
| Durable Objects  | 100,000 requests and 13,000 GB-s a day; SQLite storage only | the waiting room, later the relay |
| Outgoing traffic | not charged                                                 | —                                 |

Over a free limit, further calls of that kind fail until 00:00 UTC.

**The account service.** Assumed per device in daily use: about ten requests a day (refreshing the session, opening the waiting room's socket a few times, reading the account). Joins are rare. So the free plan's 100,000 requests fit about **10,000 devices in daily use**; requests run out first, long before the database. Assumptions to measure once built.

**The relay, after the review.** A connected device's traffic is the gateway's events: a run streams hundreds to thousands of them. Assumed: about 25,000 messages a day for a device in daily use, nearly all from the connector. Durable Objects bill incoming WebSocket messages at 20 to a request and nothing for outgoing ones, and charge no time while a socket hibernates between messages. That is about 1,300 requests a day per device, so the free plan fits about **75 devices in daily use**; at an assumed 2 ms of work per message, time (about 6 GB-s a day per device) runs out much later. Requests run out first here too. The live view of a bot's computer adds far more messages than text and would lower that.

**The person's own machine as the relay.** The connector already runs there, but a machine behind a home router can't be reached from outside without something outside: a port opened on the router (often off, and a risk), or a meeting point that helps the two ends reach each other directly (which the account service could be, cheaply) with a relay still needed when either end is behind carrier-grade address translation, as phones on cellular often are. Not in the first version; worth measuring later as the way to keep most relayed traffic off the project's bill.

**The person's own private network.** Where the gateway's machine is on a Tailscale network or the like, the connector listens there too and the card carries that address, so a device on the same network reaches the gateway from anywhere, for $0 to the project. Clawtrate never sets that network up.

### 9.3 Where it stops being free, and after

| Devices in daily use | Account service            | Relay (after the review)       |
| -------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| 100                  | $0                         | $0 to $5 a month               |
| 10,000               | $0 to $5 a month           | about $90 a month              |
| 1,000,000            | about $100 to $150 a month | about $8,000 to $9,000 a month |

From Cloudflare's paid plan: $5 a month for 10 million Worker requests ($0.30 a million after), 1 million Durable Object requests ($0.15 a million after) and 400,000 GB-s ($12.50 a million after); D1's writes beyond 50 million a month at $1 a million. Every number in this table rests on the assumptions above and is an order of magnitude, not a quote. What drives cost is relayed traffic, and within it, the computer's live view.

### 9.4 What the first version accepts

- **Limits.** Past about 10,000 devices in daily use, the service stops for the rest of each day once its free requests are spent. The $5 plan is the fix.
- **Dependence on one free plan.** If Cloudflare changes it, the service moves to the paid plan or to the self-hosted runner on a small server. Nothing about the person's data moves, because the service holds almost none, and the lists live on the devices and the connector.
- **No reach from anywhere** until the relay, which also waits for the review. When it comes, the free plan carries a small number of people; a share per connector per day keeps one heavy user from spending everyone's, and the paid plan follows use.

### 9.5 Self-hosting

The service is open source, and the same code runs as one Node process with SQLite, as #281's relay draft had a Node runner. Hosting it takes a machine with a public HTTPS address, a GitHub OAuth app and a Google OAuth client registered to that address (free, a few minutes each in their consoles), and the address set once in Clawtrate's Settings → Advanced → Account service. A self-hosted service is its own set of accounts. The relay, later, is the same.

### 9.6 When the hosted service is down

Everything that is connected or can connect keeps working: a gateway on the same computer, a typed address, SSH, and an account device over the connector's direct listener, because a device keeps its card and its gateway token, and the connector keeps its list. Nothing on a connection asks the service. Removing a device still works from Settings while connected to the gateway, because the connector also follows the gateway's own device list (§4.6). What stops: signing in a new device, joining, and the account page. After the relay ships, relayed connections stop while the relay is down; direct ones don't.

## 11. Before and after the cryptographic review

**The direct listener, before the review (proposed).** The connector listens on the gateway machine's local network and private-network addresses (never on a public address) with TLS 1.3, using Node's own TLS with a self-signed certificate whose key is pinned in the card. Each device presents its TLS client key's certificate, and the connector lets in only members. It is the HTTP end, not a pipe:

- **One request per connection.** Each TLS connection carries either one pass request (§4.4 step 6) or exactly one WebSocket upgrade, and is closed after anything else.
- **The upgrade is the connector's own.** It builds the request to the gateway itself from an allowlist of headers (`Host`, `Upgrade`, `Connection`, `Sec-WebSocket-Key`, `Sec-WebSocket-Version`, `Sec-WebSocket-Protocol`, `Sec-WebSocket-Extensions`, `User-Agent`), dropping every `Forwarded`, `X-Forwarded-*`, `X-Real-IP`, `Tailscale-*`, `Cf-Access-*` and `Remote-User` header and anything else the device sent, then sets `X-Forwarded-For` to the device's own socket address. After the upgrade it carries WebSocket messages only.
- **Never loopback.** A connection whose source address is loopback (a tunnel to the listener) is closed; the gateway would refuse it anyway (§3).
- **Proxy trust exactly loopback.** `gateway.trustedProxies` gains exactly `127.0.0.1` and `::1`. The connector refuses to link, saying why, while `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveCidrs` is set or the gateway's trusted-proxy sign-in allows loopback, because a local process could then claim an address or an identity that grants something. What it still changes: a local process can choose the address the gateway's sign-in rate limit counts against. A local process still needs a credential, and the shared token is long and random, so that costs nothing in practice; it is stated here because it is a change.

This is a standard construction and not the relay protocol, so it doesn't wait for that review. On Windows, a program that listens on the local network brings up the system's firewall prompt the first time (not established on the installer's path; §12).

| The connector's direct listener on the same network and a private network                                                                                                    | The connector's relay side                                                                          |

An interim option, not recommended: carrying the direct listener's TLS through a plain relay that only passes bytes, so a gateway in a virtual machine behind its host works before the review. It would be a second channel to build, review and keep beside the Noise one, for the months until the review; finishing the review is the shorter path.

**The account's own protocol is new cryptography** and gets its own independent review before anything built on it merges, since merging is shipping. That review covers: the signing contexts and the two keys; the join (commit, reveal, words, what the words cover, the card's hash among it, the decoys, grinding, the pause the connector keeps); the list (signatures, order, removal, restore, the list secret's rotation); the connector's passes and its checks before approving; the direct listener's request rebuilding, pinning and client certificates; and the session signed by the session key.

### The Windows half

Written to be handed over as it stands; Windows work in this repository belongs to its own contributor.

1. **Secure storage on Windows.** Electron's `safeStorage` uses DPAPI there. Without it the app has no full access, so it can hold neither `operator.admin` nor `operator.pairing`, and can neither link nor confirm. First, and needed by #629 too. DPAPI keeps secrets from other users, not from other programs of the same user (§10).
2. **Connecting on the same Windows computer.** Reading OpenClaw's settings on Windows (`%USERPROFILE%\.openclaw\openclaw.json` for a native install) without running OpenClaw's programs, and a send gate that proves the other end of the loopback socket is this account's gateway (the owning process from the TCP table, its user, its image, the profile's lock naming it), the counterpart of `apps/desktop/src/setup/local-prove.ts` (Windows has none yet, as the header of `settings-reader.ts` says). A gateway inside WSL (OpenClaw's Windows app keeps one of its own, `<openclaw>/docs/platforms/windows.md` in 2026.11.10) answers loopback through WSL's own relay, so this proof can't name it, and it needs its own design; until then the connector for such a gateway runs inside WSL as on a server (§8.4).
3. **The connector's lifetime on Windows.** Running while Clawtrate runs, and an option to start at sign-in to Windows (a per-user Scheduled Task or the Run key) so devices reach the gateway when the app is closed. No service, no administrator rights. The pass to the connector goes on its standard input, as on macOS.
4. **The firewall.** The direct listener on the local network: whether the installer adds a rule for the connector (it already runs with the rights to) or the system prompts on first listen, and what the person sees either way. Not established (§14).
5. **The sign-in return.** The system browser opening and returning to `http://127.0.0.1:<port>/callback` on Windows, through the default browser, with the loopback port chosen by the app.
6. **Checks.** The connector's pairing, the join, the pass and the connector's approvals against a development gateway on Windows, as the macOS checks will run them.
