# A Clawtrate account: adding a device by signing in

How a person links their OpenClaw gateway to a Clawtrate account once, on the gateway's machine, and then adds any other computer or phone by signing in to that account, with no pairing code, no SSH and no command (issue #638). It joins the remote case of #629, the phone of #14 and #286, and the relay of #254. This is a design for sign-off: nothing hosted is built until it is signed off.

**Established** marks what the repository or OpenClaw's own source and documentation show today. **Proposed** marks this design's choice. **Not established** marks what could not be shown here, with what would show it. OpenClaw facts are from the published packages `openclaw@2026.10.6` (the version Clawtrate is tested against) and `openclaw@2026.11.10`, read only; `<openclaw>/docs/…` and `<openclaw>/dist/…` are paths inside either package, and line numbers in `dist/` are 2026.10.6's (chunk names carry a build hash; 2026.11.10 has the same rules in its own chunks).

This version takes in an independent security review. Its findings are in the text where they apply; "What changed since the first draft" below lists them.

## The page to decide from

### What an account is for

An account lets a person's devices find their gateway and lets their own devices say who else gets in. A person links their gateway to the account once, with one click on the gateway's machine. After that, a new computer or phone signs in with GitHub or Google, the person confirms it on a device they already have by picking the words the new device shows, and it is in: no address, no token, no code, no Terminal. Removing a device takes it out of the gateway at once when it is done on a device connected to the gateway, and as soon as the gateway's machine receives it otherwise. Someone who never makes an account keeps everything that works today.

### Linking a gateway and adding a device, step by step

The case this is for: OpenClaw runs on a Windows PC, and the person wants to use it from a MacBook.

**On the gateway's machine, once** (the Windows PC):

1. Install Clawtrate and open it. "Where does OpenClaw run?" → **This computer** → **Connect**. Clawtrate connects with full control and no approval, because OpenClaw lets a program on its own computer pair without asking (the same-computer flow of #629; on Windows it needs the Windows half of §12). _1 click._ The one exception: on a gateway whose owner turned silent local pairing off (`gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveLocal: false`), this first connection needs one approval outside Clawtrate, once, on OpenClaw's own page or by its approve command, because nothing already paired can approve it (`setup-effortless.md` §12). Everything after it is as counted.
2. Clawtrate offers "Use this gateway from your other devices". **Link with GitHub** or **Link with Google**. _1 click._ Behind it, Clawtrate starts its connector and lets it into the gateway itself; nothing is shown or asked, and the connection line may blink once while the gateway takes the change.
3. The browser opens on the account page. **Continue with Google** (or GitHub), and the provider's own consent screen the first time. _1 or 2 clicks._ If the browser isn't signed in to Google or GitHub yet, the person signs in there as they always do; that is typed into the provider, not into Clawtrate.
4. Back in Clawtrate: "Linked to your account. Sign in on your other devices." _0 clicks._

**On each other device, after** (the MacBook):

5. Install Clawtrate and open it. **Sign in**. _1 click._
6. The browser: **Continue with Google**. _1 click._
7. The Mac shows three words, for example "otter · maple · drum", and "Confirm on a device you already have".
8. On the Windows PC (or any device already in), Clawtrate shows "A device that says it is a MacBook Pro wants to use this gateway. Only pick if it is in front of you now." First come **I didn't start this** and **None of these match**, then five sets of words. The person picks the set the Mac shows. _1 click._ Picking is what checks that the two screens are talking to each other and to nobody in between.
9. The Mac says "Confirmed on Windows PC" and connects, and the person's bots, tasks, conversations and pinned bots are there. _0 clicks._ Until the relay ships, this needs the two on the same network or the same private network (§8.2).

**Count.** Gateway's machine: 3 to 4 clicks once, then 1 click for each device added. Each new device: 2 clicks. **Typed into Clawtrate: nothing. Terminal: never,** except in two cases: a gateway whose owner turned silent local pairing off, where the first connection on its machine needs that one approval outside Clawtrate (step 1), and a server with no screen (below). Today the same case needs SSH or commands on the Windows PC, and approvals that are each a command typed there (#629).

A gateway on a server with no screen is the other exception: installing the connector there is one command, once, and the first device is confirmed by typing the number of its words in that same terminal, once (§8.4), because there is no app to click.

### What the account never holds

The account never holds conversations, gateway tokens, provider credentials, the content of any file, or the gateway's private address. It holds who is signed in, which gateway is linked, and the public keys of the devices on it. It sees the public internet address of each network the person's devices and gateway use, as any service they connect to does (§5).

**Why the service can't add a device by itself:** the program on the gateway's machine lets in only a device that one of the person's own devices has signed for, with a key used for nothing else, and those keys never leave the devices, so the account service, or anyone who takes it over, has nothing to sign with (§4).

### The decisions, with a recommendation and what each costs

1. **Where it runs, for $0.** _Recommended:_ the first version runs only the account service, on Cloudflare's free plan (Workers, with its D1 database and Durable Objects for the waiting room of a join), and carries no conversation through anything the project pays for: devices reach the gateway over the person's own network, their own private network (Tailscale and the like) or SSH. The free plan's 100,000 requests a day fit roughly **10,000 devices in daily use**. Past that, Cloudflare's paid plan is $5 a month and fits roughly 30,000; a million devices cost on the order of $100 to $150 a month. What this accepts: the service stops for the rest of the day (until 00:00 UTC) if the free limits run out, it depends on a provider's free plan that can change, and every connection still works while it is down. The relay that reaches a gateway from anywhere comes after the cryptographic review; on the same free plan it fits only about **75 devices in daily use**, about $90 a month at 10,000 and $8,000 to $9,000 a month at a million (§9). The code is open source and the same service runs on any small machine for someone who hosts it themselves.
2. **Sign-in.** _Decided:_ GitHub and Google first. The account service is the only program registered with each provider; the apps never hold a provider's secret or token, and the service reads the provider's answer once for the person's id and keeps no provider token (§7). _A third, signing in with a ChatGPT account:_ OpenAI's [Sign in with ChatGPT](https://developers.openai.com/siwc/quickstart) exists, but its identity sign-in is "currently available to selected commercial partners through a limited trial" (read 2026-11-25), so it needs OpenAI to admit Clawtrate first; adding it then is one provider module, not a redesign.
3. **Confirming a new device.** _Recommended:_ pick the matching words on a device already in (one click when it is right). It is what keeps the service from admitting anyone. A sign-in alone admitting a device would mean whoever controls the service, or the person's Google or GitHub session, could get into their gateway; that option is laid out in §4.7 and not recommended. A passkey can later drop the click on devices that support it.
4. **"The same on every device."** _Recommended for the first version:_ conversations, tasks and routines (already on the gateway), the rail's pins, sections and hidden bots (already there), read and unread, the theme and the gateway's name follow the person, kept on their own gateway. Switches that hide what bots do (Show commands, Show thinking, other tools' sessions, the gateway's own bot) follow only when turned on, never off, because anything with write access to the gateway can change that store. The run limit, "New bots too", drafts and the window's layout stay on each device (§6).
5. **One gateway per account in the first version.** Linking a second needs one of the person's devices to sign for it. This keeps someone with a stolen sign-in from linking their own gateway and catching the person's next device (§10).

### What waits for the relay's cryptographic review

**Ships before it:** the account service, signing in, linking a gateway, the device list, confirming and removing devices, the gateway letting a confirmed device in with no approval typed, the settings that follow a person, and reaching the gateway over the same network, a private network or SSH. None of these carries a conversation through the relay. The account's own signing protocol is new and gets its own independent review before anything built on it merges (§11).

**Waits for it:** reaching a gateway from anywhere through Clawtrate's relay (a phone on cellular, a gateway in a virtual machine behind its host's network, a laptop away from home), because that is what the review is of. `docs/decisions.md` says nothing builds on the relay protocol until the review has passed, and this design keeps that: nothing in the account imports `packages/relay-protocol`.

### What changed since the first draft

- **Each new device gets a pass to the gateway of its own**, made by the connector and handed only to that device over its own connection, because the gateway refuses a device that brings no credential at all. Nothing changes on screen (§4.4).
- **The connector lets itself into the gateway with a pass the app makes**, so it never needs the gateway's shared token, for the same reason (§4.3).
- **Signing to the account service and signing for a new device use separate keys and labels**, so the service can never get a device to sign "let this one in" by asking it to sign something else (§4.2).
- **The connector writes every request it passes to the gateway itself**, so a device can never pass as a program on the gateway's own computer and skip the checks (§11).
- **The confirmation shows five sets of words, after "I didn't start this" and "None of these match"**, and the gateway's own machine, not the service, enforces the pause after a refusal. It stays one click when it is right (§4.4).
- **An account has one gateway in the first version**, so someone with a stolen sign-in can't put their own gateway in front of the person (§10).
- **Removing a stolen device also offers to remove everything it added**, and the account page can't remove the gateway's own computer or its connector (§4.6).
- **Only what can't hide anything follows a person through the gateway** (§6).
- **The join was run on development gateways of both OpenClaw versions, and works.** A new device needs one approval, which the gateway's machine gives by itself, not two; nothing changes on screen and no count above changes. The runs also showed that the connector must check what each device first sends, and it does (§4.5).
- **A gateway on a server with no screen gets its first device at the server's own terminal:** the person types the number of the new device's words there, once, so the service still can't admit anyone (§8.4).
- **What the service sees is stated exactly:** public addresses, the provider's answer for a moment, and its platform's logs (§5).

## 1. The promise, in a person's words

These are the sentences the screens and the website may say. Each rests on a section below.

- **Linking.** "Link this gateway to your Clawtrate account. You do this once, on the computer OpenClaw runs on." One click there, then the provider's sign-in. An account has one gateway. Linking changes nothing in how the gateway works for anything already connected to it.
- **Adding a device.** "Sign in on your other computer or phone, then confirm it on a device you already have." The new device shows three words; the person picks them on the other device. From then on the new device reconnects by itself, with nothing to sign in to again until it is removed.
- **Removing a device.** "Remove it from Settings on any of your devices, or from your account page in a browser if you no longer have any." It loses access to the gateway as soon as the connector hears of it: at once from a device that is connected to the gateway, or, from the account page or a device that isn't connected to the gateway, when the connector next receives it through the account service, with the page showing the removal as waiting until the connector confirms it. Until then the device keeps its access. Its saved credentials then stop working. Clawtrate offers to remove what it added too.
- **Unlinking.** "Unlink on the gateway's computer, or from any of your devices." The devices the account added are removed from the gateway too, unless the person keeps them (§8.5).
- **Without an account.** "You don't need an account to use Clawtrate." Connecting on the same computer, a typed address, SSH and setup codes all work as they do today, and an account is never asked for.
- **When the service is down.** "Your devices keep working with your gateway." Only signing in a new device and the account page need the service.

## 3. What OpenClaw offers, established first

The account can only do what OpenClaw lets a program on the gateway's machine do. `docs/design/setup-effortless.md` (§2, §3 and §5) established much of this live on OpenClaw 2026.10.6; this section keeps what this design rests on and adds what it found. A probe then ran the join, the connector's own pairing and the header cases on development gateways of both versions (A3, §12); **ran, both versions** marks what it showed.

**Every connection must bring a credential before pairing is considered** (established, both versions). In the gateway's token or password mode, a hello that carries none of the shared secret, a bootstrap token or a device token is refused and its socket closed before any pairing request is filed (`<openclaw>/dist/message-handler-*.mjs` lines 1263 to 1269; `auth-*.mjs` lines 280 to 291; `node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 70 to 160, where only a bootstrap or device token can make the hello authorized without the shared secret). A device's signature covers the token it presents (`node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 211 to 219), so nothing in between can add one to a device's hello. So a new device can't just "ask" the gateway: it needs a credential of its own first.

**A bootstrap token is that credential: a pass bound to the first key that presents it** (ran, both versions). `device.pair.setupCode` (needs `operator.admin`) issues a setup code whose payload is base64url JSON holding the address, a bootstrap token and its expiry (`<openclaw>/dist/setup-code-*.mjs` lines 188 and 230 to 300). A gateway bound to loopback makes one only when the caller gives an address (`publicUrl`); its own loopback `ws://` address is accepted, and then the code carries the default ("full") profile (`setup-code-*.mjs` lines 54 to 75, 186 and 275 to 277). Its parameters name no device, so a pass can't be made for a key in advance.

- **What it allows.** The default profile's operator scopes are `operator.admin`, `operator.read`, `operator.write`, `operator.approvals`, `operator.questions` and `operator.talk.secrets`; `bootstrapProfile: "limited"` gives the same without admin (`device-bootstrap-profile-*.mjs`). No profile names `operator.pairing`, but `operator.admin` satisfies every operator scope (`operator-scope-compat-*.mjs` lines 10 to 13), so a first hello with a default pass may ask for the six, and one approval gives a device that approves other devices.
- **How long, and how often.** It lasts 10 minutes (`DEVICE_BOOTSTRAP_TOKEN_TTL_MS`, `device-pairing-identity-*.mjs` line 24). On first use it is bound to that device's id and public key, and any other key presenting it is refused (`device-bootstrap.worker-kernel-*.mjs` lines 230 to 290). It is used up only when every role in its profile has been redeemed, and every profile names `node` too, so a Clawtrate client, which redeems only `operator`, can present it again with the same key until it expires (`message-handler-*.mjs` lines 2500 to 2522). There is no call to revoke it.
- **When it waits.** A pass presented on a remote connection, or with `autoApproveLocal: false`, files a pending request that someone must approve. On a direct loopback socket every client pairs silently with it.
- **Who can skip the approval.** A pass presented by a client that says it is OpenClaw's own iOS or Android app in node mode is let in by the pass alone, remotely too, and given an operator token with the profile's scopes, admin included on a default pass (`isSetupCodeHandoffBootstrapClient`, `message-handler-*.mjs` lines 834 to 836 and 1940 to 1958). The gateway checks only the client's stated id, platform and device family, which the client chooses. #286's research (its §2) found the same. §4.5 says how the connector closes it.

**A program on the gateway's own computer can pair without approval** (established, both versions). A first pairing over loopback with no forwarded headers and no browser `Origin` is approved silently with whatever its credential allows, unless `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveLocal` is `false` (default true): `shouldAllowSilentLocalPairing` in `node-connect-reconcile-*.mjs` lines 178 to 185, used at `message-handler-*.mjs` lines 1894 to 1902; `<openclaw>/docs/gateway/pairing.md`, "Silent local pairing" (2026.10.6 line 229, 2026.11.10 line 291). **A device token never asks for more by reconnecting** (ran, both versions). A reconnect on a device token that asks for scopes the token doesn't hold is refused (`AUTH_SCOPE_MISMATCH`) and files nothing (`device-pairing-core.worker-*.mjs` lines 584 to 600), whatever OpenClaw's own documentation says of upgrades. An upgrade is filed only by `device.scopes.requestUpgrade` from inside a connection the device already holds, and collected with `device.scopes.waitUpgrade` (`devices-*.mjs` lines 37 to 139), which is Clawtrate's existing `requestAccess` path. A device whose grant includes `operator.admin` already holds the six, so it has nothing to upgrade.

**A paired device can approve another device** (established, both versions; `<openclaw>/docs/gateway/operator-scopes.md`, 2026.10.6 lines 531 and 597 to 610, 2026.11.10 lines 866 and 922 to 945; `dist/devices-*.mjs` lines 355 to 366). `device.pair.approve` needs `operator.pairing`, and for each scope the request asks for, that scope or `operator.admin`; a request for `operator.admin` needs admin. A device whose token lacks admin can manage only its own entry. So the connector must hold `operator.pairing` and `operator.admin`. Approval issues a token for every role the request named (`device-pairing-core.worker-*.mjs` line 117).

**A pending request is bound to the device's key, and a replayed hello files nothing** (established, both versions). The gateway derives the device id from the public key (`message-handler-*.mjs` lines 952 to 956) and checks the hello's signature over this connection's nonce within a bounded clock skew (line 958 on). The pending record keeps the public key, roles, scopes and remote address (`device-pairing-identity-*.mjs` lines 241 to 283) and expires after 5 minutes (line 602). Approval goes by request id. So the connector doesn't need to know which connection a request came from: matching the request's public key to the device list is enough (§4.5).

**How the gateway decides a connection is local** (established, both versions). A loopback socket with no `Forwarded`, `X-Forwarded-*`, `X-Real-IP` or Tailscale headers is "direct-local"; one with them is trusted only when its socket address is in `gateway.trustedProxies`, and then its client address comes from the headers; a chain that resolves to loopback is refused; anything else with those headers is refused as unattributable (`ingress-attribution-*.mjs` lines 95 to 118; `net-*.mjs` lines 37 to 47). This is decided per HTTP request, not per socket (lines 119 to 124). The client address is the rightmost `X-Forwarded-For` hop that is neither loopback nor a trusted proxy; `Forwarded` is never read for an address. Ran, both versions, with `trustedProxies` at exactly loopback: **a forwarded request that carries no forwarded header is direct-local and pairs silently**; one whose `X-Forwarded-For` the device chose is remote, with the device's chosen address; `Forwarded`, `X-Real-IP` or `X-Forwarded-User` alone, or a loopback `X-Forwarded-For`, is refused (403). Once loopback is a trusted proxy, any local process can claim a client address; in token or password mode it is then treated as remote, still needs a credential, and chooses only the address its request records and its sign-in rate limit counts against. `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveCidrs` ignores addresses that arrive through a loopback proxy (`message-handler-*.mjs` lines 128 to 147), and a forwarded device in its range stayed pending (ran, both versions). In trusted-proxy sign-in with `allowLoopback`, by contrast, a forwarded `X-Forwarded-User` header is a credential: a device with no other credential got past sign-in and filed a request (ran, both versions). §11 says what the connector does about all of this. **Changing proxy trust doesn't restart the gateway, but closes every open connection** (ran, both versions): the gateway reloads it in place and then closes every WebSocket within about 0.3 to 3.6 seconds, the connector's and Clawtrate's own included, which reconnect at once. Removing entries from it later needs `replacePaths`, or the gateway refuses the change.

**What the gateway listens on** (established). Loopback by default on port 18789; `lan`, `tailnet`, `custom` or `auto` binds need gateway authentication; its own TLS; Tailscale Serve (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/config-gateway.md`, 2026.10.6 line 145, 2026.11.10 line 146). Changing the bind or TLS restarts the gateway under the default reload mode `hybrid` (the same file, 2026.10.6 lines 330 and 359 to 362). **Proposed:** the design changes none of these. The gateway stays on loopback; the connector does the listening (§11).

**What a plugin can do** (established for 2026.11.10, read only). The principal-bound remote ingress a plugin can open is offered "only for bundled plugins and verified official installations" (`<openclaw>/docs/plugins/sdk-gateway-ingress.md`), so a Clawtrate plugin can't be the network entrance. The connector is an ordinary client on loopback instead, which needs nothing installed into the gateway.

**Who a device is to the gateway's per-person settings** (established, both versions). Every operator connection without a verified user gets the gateway's owner profile while `gateway.roles` is unset (`gateway-owner-profile-*.mjs` lines 46 and 47), so two of a person's devices paired by device token are the same user for `users.prefs`. With `gateway.roles` set, a device-token connection gets `no_durable_identity` and nothing follows it (`users-*.mjs` lines 338 to 347). `users.prefs.set` needs `operator.write` on 2026.10.6 (`core-method-policy-*.mjs` lines 2434 to 2438) and only `operator.read` on 2026.11.10, so there any read-only device can change it (ran, both versions). No tool a bot holds can call it: the gateway tool's actions are configuration reads and updates only, on both versions (read from source).

**What OpenClaw itself has for remote access** (established, 2026.11.10 only). An experimental, off-by-default Lab, "OpenClaw ID remote access" (`<openclaw>/docs/concepts/experimental-features.md`; `<openclaw>/docs/cli/id.md`), keeps owner devices certified by a root key derived from a recovery kit, and a discovery service that "can relay a pending request but cannot admit" (`<openclaw>/docs/gateway/remote.md` line 74). It is not in 2026.10.6 and needs the Lab turned on and its own sign-in. This design does not depend on it and changes nothing it does.

## 4. How a signed-in device is let in

### 4.1 Who decides

The gateway decides whether a device is paired, as everywhere in Clawtrate. **Proposed:** the party that asks the gateway to approve an account device is the **connector**, on the gateway's machine, using the gateway's own `device.pair.approve` under its own `operator.pairing` and `operator.admin`. It makes a pass for a device and approves its request only when that device is on the gateway's **device list**, and a device is on the list only when one of the person's own devices signed for it after the person picked the matching words. So the decision is the person's twice over: they linked the connector on their machine, and they confirmed each device on another of their own.

The account service introduces devices to each other and keeps a waiting room for a join. It holds no key that can sign for a device, and the connector believes nothing the service says that would let someone in. The rule throughout: **anything that grants access must be signed by one of the person's devices; anything that only takes access away may come from the service too, except for the gateway machine's own device and the connector (§4.6).**

This deliberately differs from "the account vouches" in the wording of #638: an account that vouches by itself is an account whose operator can let anyone in (§4.7).

Never reused for the relay's registration later; that gets its own key. |
| Connector TLS key             | the connector                             | Its direct listener's certificate, pinned by every device from the card (§11).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| List secret (32 random bytes) | the connector and each device on the list | Sealing the list's entries and the gateway's card, so the service carries them without reading them.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Seal key (X25519)             | each device, in its secure storage        | Opening what is sealed to that device alone: the list secret and card at its join, and its share of each rotation. Kept apart from the list key, which only signs.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |

**Signing contexts.** Every signed object starts with its own fixed label, `clawtrate account <kind> v1` followed by a newline, for each kind: session request, list entry, refusal, card, join transcript, list secret (a rotation), list head. `removal notice` is the label of the account page's unsigned removal request, which nothing signs (§4.6). A signature checked under one label never verifies under another. A device never signs bytes the service chose: the only service-supplied value it signs is a challenge nonce, inside a session request whose structure the device builds itself. Two keys rather than one with labels are kept on purpose, so a bug in one kind can't reach the other. The account's protocol package imports nothing from `packages/relay-protocol`, which hasn't had its review; it borrows the idea of a label in front of a signed payload, not the code.

#### 4.2.1 The list secret: first, after a removal, and who vouches for it

- **The first secret** is made by the connector at linking and handed to the linking app on the gateway's machine over the local channel between the two (the connector's standard input and output, as the pass in §4.3), never through the service. On a server with no app, the first device gets it inside the first join's confirmation, which the connector signs there (§8.4).
- **A joining device** gets the current secret sealed to its seal key inside the member's signed confirmation (§4.4 step 5).
- **After a removal** the connector makes a new secret with the next epoch and publishes a **rotation**: one share per remaining device, each sealed to that device's seal key, the whole signed by the connector. A device takes a rotation only when the connector's signature verifies and its epoch is newer than the secret it holds. The connector is the right signer: it is the authority that holds the list and has just carried out the removal on the gateway, it is always there when a removal takes effect, and anyone who controls it already controls the gateway's machine. A removed device gets no share, and nothing sealed afterwards opens with its old secret.

#### 4.2.2 Who knows the latest list: the head

A list cut short still verifies from its first entry, so a service could show a member, or a new device opening its first list, an old state with a removal hidden. So the connector signs a **head** with every change to the list: the gateway, the account, the number of entries, the latest entry's hash and the current epoch. Every device keeps the newest head it has seen and refuses a list, or a head, older than it.

- A device connected to the gateway through the direct listener reads the head from the connector itself, so the service can't hold it back.
- A device that can't reach the connector takes heads through the service, never older than its own, and shows the device list as "as of <time>: your gateway couldn't be reached to check it". It can still send a removal; it doesn't treat a device as still listed, or as removed, on that state alone.
- A new device's first head is the one its words covered (§4.4 step 5); it reads the connector's own as soon as it connects.

#### 4.2.3 Constructions, as built in the protocol package (#651), subject to its cryptographic review

### 4.3 Linking, on the gateway's machine

**With Clawtrate on that machine** (it already holds a full pairing from the same-computer flow):

1. The person clicks **Link**.
2. Clawtrate reads the gateway's settings over its admin connection and refuses to link, saying why, while the gateway uses trusted-proxy sign-in that allows loopback: there a forwarded identity header is a credential, so the connector's checks would stop being the only way in (§3, §11). `autoApproveCidrs` is not a reason to refuse: it ignores every device the connector forwards (§3).
3. Clawtrate asks the gateway for a setup code (`device.pair.setupCode`, with the gateway's own loopback address as `publicUrl`), checks that it carries the default profile, takes the bootstrap token out of it in memory, and gives only the token to the connector it starts, on the connector's standard input: never in a command line, environment, file or log.
4. The connector, with a new identity, sends one hello straight to the gateway on loopback, with no forwarded headers, as role `operator`, asking for the six, with the pass. OpenClaw pairs it silently as a local first pairing, with the six (ran, both versions). Where `autoApproveLocal` is `false`, the request waits instead, and Clawtrate approves exactly that request: its request id and the connector's public key, which Clawtrate knows because it started the connector; the connector's next hello with the same pass gets the six. Either way there is no second step. From then on the connector connects on its device token, asking for the six. It has never seen the gateway's shared token.
5. The connector sets `gateway.trustedProxies` to include exactly `127.0.0.1` and `::1` through the gateway's config call, recording the earlier value and the value it wrote. The gateway applies it without a restart but closes every open connection, Clawtrate's own and the connector's included, within a few seconds (§3); both treat that close as a reconnect, not an error, and the person sees the connection line blink at most. On unlink it takes out only the entries it added (the config call's `replacePaths`), and only while the setting still holds exactly the value it wrote; if anything else changed it since, it leaves the setting as it is and says so, as #286's design does over a shell.
6. The connector listens for the gateway's `device.pair.requested` and `device.pair.resolved` events, and reads `device.pair.list` on every reconnect, so it never needs to poll (§4.5).
7. The browser signs in to the account. The account service records the link: this gateway's random id, the connector's public key and the sealed card. If the account already has a gateway, the link is refused unless a device on that gateway's list signs for it (§10).
8. The connector writes the list's first entry, signed by its key and by the linking app's list key: those two are the list's first members, and they are protected (§4.6). It hands the app the first list secret over their local channel (§4.2.1) and signs the list's first head. This happens on the gateway's machine and involves the service only as a mailbox.

The person sees one click and the provider's sign-in (§8.2). Everything from step 2 to step 8 is automatic, and it takes one approval at most, by Clawtrate itself, only when the gateway's owner turned local auto-approval off.

**On a machine without Clawtrate** (a server): the connector reads the gateway's shared token from OpenClaw's own settings file, as Clawtrate's same-computer connect does (`setup-effortless.md` §3), uses it once for a first hello on loopback asking for the six, which OpenClaw pairs silently, and then forgets it. Where `autoApproveLocal` is `false`, the connector prints the request id and the gateway's approve command once, in the same terminal the person installed it from. Its list has no device member until the first join, confirmed at that terminal (§8.4).

Every gateway call above ran on development gateways of both versions (A3).

### 4.4 Adding a device: the join

The new device **N** is signed in. An existing member **A** is any device on the list, including Clawtrate on the gateway's machine. The service carries every message of steps 1 to 5 and can read none of what matters.

1. N makes its keys and asks to join the account's gateway. It first sends only a **commitment**: a hash of its public keys and a random value, so it can't change them later.
2. Each member that is open is told and shows the request. A member that is shown two joins at once refuses both and says so; that ends both joins and does not pause joining (§4.5), since a service could show two joins on purpose. The first member the person uses answers with its own public key, the list's latest entry, a hash of the gateway's current card (the connector's keys, its TLS pin and its addresses, as signed by the connector) and a random value of its own.
3. N then sends its public keys and its random value. Both sides compute three words from everything exchanged. Because N committed first and A answered before seeing N's keys, someone in the middle (the service, say) can't choose keys that make the words match: each attempt has about one chance in 8.6 billion (three words from a list of 2,048).
4. N shows its words. A shows, first and with the same weight, **I didn't start this** and **None of these match**, then five sets of words: the right one and four A makes with its own randomness, sharing no word with it. Above them: "A device that says it is <name> wants to use this gateway. Only pick if it is in front of you now." The name is whatever the device says, shown with the platform from its record; names refuse control, bidirectional and invisible (default-ignorable) characters, but look-alike letters remain possible, so the screen never treats the name as proof: the words are. The person picks. **The pick is the confirmation.** When someone in the middle has changed the keys, none of the five is what N shows. Someone who picks one of the five sets without looking at the new device is right one time in five; someone who presses any of the seven choices at random, one time in seven. The decoys are uniform words, so a person who has seen the right words always finds them.
5. A right pick: A signs an entry adding N (its list key, TLS client key, gateway device public key, name and platform), seals the list secret and the gateway's card to N, and sends both through the service. The connector checks the entry's signature against the list it holds and appends it. N checks, before it trusts A or uses the card: that the confirmation is signed by the list key the words covered; that the card's hash is the one A sent in step 2, which the words covered; then it opens the sealed list, verifies it from its first entry, and checks that its latest entry is the head the words covered, that A is a current device member on it, and that the card is signed by the connector named in its first entry. Anything that fails ends the join. Then N says "Confirmed on <A's name>".
   A wrong pick, "None of these match" or "I didn't start this": A signs a refusal naming the join's commitment and the list's head, and sends it to the connector, which then accepts no entry adding a device for an hour and tells every member. The connector takes each refusal once: only one naming its current head, and never the same one twice, so a refusal replayed by the service can't keep joining paused. "I didn't start this" also tells the person to secure their Google or GitHub account. The pause is enforced by the connector on the gateway's machine, so the service can't lift it.
6. **N gets its pass.** N opens a TLS connection to the connector's direct listener (§11) with its client certificate. The connector checks that N is on the list, has no pairing yet and has no pass outstanding, asks the gateway for a setup code on its admin connection, takes the bootstrap token out in memory, and returns only the token to N on that connection. The token never passes through the service and is written nowhere; the gateway binds it to the first device that uses it, which is N. It can't be revoked and isn't used up by N's pairing, so the connector keeps, for its 10 minutes, a record of each pass outstanding: the device it was made for and when it expires.
7. **N's first pairing.** N opens a second TLS connection and sends a WebSocket upgrade, which the connector rebuilds as its own (§11). The connector reads N's first message before passing it on and lets through only Clawtrate's own hello from N's own key (§4.5). N's hello presents the token, as role `operator`, asking for the six. The gateway accepts it as a bootstrap sign-in from a remote device and files a pending request. The connector approves it under §4.5's checks. N's next hello with the same pass, which Clawtrate already retries every few seconds, gets its device token straight from the gateway.
8. **N's full control.** From then on N connects on its device token, asking for the six, which its grant covers: `operator.admin` satisfies `operator.pairing` (§3). There is no second request and no second approval. N holds the six, as a device paired the ordinary way does.

Steps 6 to 8 ran on development gateways of both versions (A3): one approval, three hellos, a fraction of a second when the approval is immediate. Nothing here changes what the gateway's scopes allow: a device that asked for less gets less. Clawtrate's client must never reconnect asking for more than its token holds, which the gateway refuses as final (`AUTH_SCOPE_MISMATCH`); a device paired with less upgrades through `device.scopes.requestUpgrade`, as today. Its retries outlast the pending request's 5 minutes by sending a new hello, which files a new request while the pass lasts.

**Default pass or limited pass.** The connector asks for the default pass, which carries admin. A limited pass (no admin) would need a second approval, through `device.scopes.requestUpgrade`, and would protect little: a leaked limited pass still yields write by the pose of §3. What closes the pose is the door and the reconciliation of §4.5, which hold for either; so the default pass, with one approval, is the choice.

### 4.5 What the connector checks (proposed, on facts that ran)

A pass holder can be paired without anyone's approval by saying it is OpenClaw's own phone app (§3). The only holder of a pass is a device a member just confirmed, so that would give it nothing it wasn't about to be given, except a pairing under a key the list doesn't know, which would survive the device's removal. The door stops it on the only path a pass can travel, and the reconciliation removes anything that gets past the door, so a mistake in one is caught by the other.

**At the door, on every forwarded connection.** The connector carries the WebSocket messages itself, so it reads the first one the device sends, after passing on the gateway's `connect.challenge`, before passing anything on. It passes it on only when all of these hold, and otherwise closes the connection without sending it:

- it is a text message, at most the gateway's frame limit, that parses as the gateway's `connect` request (`type` `req`, `method` `connect`);
- `params.client.id` is `gateway-client` and `params.client.mode` is `ui`, Clawtrate's own;
- `params.role` is `operator`, and `params.scopes` are within the six;
- `params.device.publicKey` is the gateway device key on the entry of the member whose client certificate opened this TLS connection;
- `params.auth` holds a bootstrap token only while that member has a pass outstanding and no pairing, and a device token otherwise, never a shared token or password.

A binary message, a message that isn't a `connect`, or anything before it closes the connection. After the gateway's `hello-ok`, the connector passes messages both ways, reading only enough of each one the device sends to close the connection on a second `connect` (not established: whether the gateway already refuses one, §14). So a pass can't be presented through the connector under another key or as another client, and the gateway is reachable only on loopback, where only the connector and Clawtrate ever hold a pass.

**Before every approval.**

- The pending request's **public key** (not only its device id) is exactly the gateway device key of a current member's entry.
- Its roles are exactly `["operator"]`: approval issues a token for every role named (§3).
- Its scopes are within the six, and its client is `gateway-client`.
- That member has a pass outstanding.
- It **reads the request again immediately before approving**, and approves by its request id with nothing in between.
- It **rejects** every pending request of a key on no current entry while one of its passes is outstanding, and leaves every other pending request for the person, in Clawtrate's Devices pane as today.

**And after, the reconciliation.** On every `device.pair.resolved` event and on every reconnect, the connector compares the gateway's paired devices with the list. A pairing whose key is on no entry and that the gateway approved by a pass (`approvedVia: "bootstrap"`) while one of the connector's passes was outstanding is removed at once (`device.pair.remove`, then `device.token.revoke`), and every member is told: only a pass could have made it, and every pass the connector made belongs to a listed key. Every other pairing not on the list is shown, not removed (§4.6), since the person may have paired it the ordinary way.

**Refusals and the pause.** The connector pauses entries that add a device for an hour on a refusal for a wrong pick, "None of these match" or "I didn't start this", taking each refusal once and only when it names the current head. A refusal because two joins were shown at once ends those joins and doesn't pause. The pause is kept on the gateway's machine; the service can neither lift it nor stretch it.

### 4.6 Removing a device

From Settings → Devices on any member, or the account page in a browser:

1. A member signs a removal entry (a list entry recording that a member removed it). The account page, which has no list key, sends an unsigned removal request (the `removal notice`), which the connector writes into the list as a removal entry it signs, recording that it came from the account page. The connector accepts an unsigned removal for any member **except** the gateway machine's own device and the connector itself, which only a member's signature or an action on the gateway's machine can remove. Unlinking is the same.
2. The connector removes the device's pairing on the gateway (`device.pair.remove`, then `device.token.revoke` for any token left), rejects its pending requests, closes its connections, drops its keys from the listener, writes a new list secret and seals it to the members that remain. The device's saved credentials stop working at once. A removal from a member connected to the gateway reaches the connector directly; one from the account page, or signed on a member that isn't connected to the gateway, waits in the service until the connector, which checks for removals every minute while it can reach the service, takes it, and the page shows it as waiting until then. The connector being unable to reach the account service doesn't stop its direct listener: a device on the list keeps its access through the listener, and a removal sent through the service waits. **So a stolen device keeps its access until a removal reaches the connector.** What shortens that: removing it from a device connected to the gateway, which reaches the connector directly, or on the gateway's machine itself. A device with its own route (a typed address, SSH) is removed from Settings on a member connected to the gateway, as today.
3. **What it added goes too, by default.** Removing a device offers, ticked, to also remove every entry it signed and every gateway pairing that appeared while it was a member and isn't on the list.
4. **Every unsigned removal is announced** on every member, with **Put it back**. A member signs the restore, and the device pairs again with a new pass (§4.4 steps 6 to 8), without new words. Only a removal the account page made can be put back: a device a member removed on purpose joins again with new keys and new words, and the list refuses keys it has held before.
5. **The connector watches the gateway's own device list both ways.** A device removed elsewhere (Clawtrate's Devices pane, OpenClaw's own page or command) leaves the list too. A paired device that is not on the list and wasn't paired when the gateway was linked is shown on every member as "Paired outside your account", with Remove, so nothing a thief or anyone else added stays hidden.
6. A pairing a pass made under a key on no entry is removed by the reconciliation of §4.5, not just shown.

A device with full control can change the gateway's settings too (its bind, its sign-in, its proxy trust). Removing it ends its access; it doesn't undo what it changed. §10 says what the person checks.

## 5. What the account holds and never holds

**Never holds:** conversations, transcripts, files, tasks, routines, memory, gateway tokens (shared, device or bootstrap), provider credentials or keys, the list secret, the content of the gateway's card (its private addresses, its name, its certificate), any device's private key, any device's name.

**Receives for a moment, never stores:** the sign-in provider's token. The service uses it once to read the person's id, display name and picture address, and then discards it; it is never written or logged. GitHub's tokens for OAuth apps don't expire, so the service revokes each one through GitHub's API right after reading. The Google token is discarded and expires within the hour; no refresh token is asked for.

One-time codes and challenges go in request bodies, never in an address, so request logs can't carry them.

**What the service necessarily sees:** who signs in, when, and the public internet address they come from; the public address of the gateway's network, because the connector reaches the service from there and the linking browser signs in from there, and when the connector is online; how many devices each account has; when a device joins or is removed; the sizes and times of what it carries. It does not see the gateway's private address, port or name, or which devices talk to the gateway and when.

**The account page shows** each device's platform, when it was added and the day it was last seen, and not its name: names are sealed with the list secret, which the page doesn't have. Clawtrate's own Settings show the names.

**What a person can delete:** any device (§4.6); the linked gateway (unlinking); a sign-in method, while another remains; the whole account, from the account page, which removes every row at once and asks the connector to remove the devices the account added. Backups held by the hosting provider expire with its restore window, at most 30 days (not established: the exact window of the chosen plan, §14).

**The relay, later,** sees what #286's design lists in its §3: the addresses of both ends, when each connects, sizes and timing, and a random id for the connector. It keeps none of what is said.

## 6. The same on every device

Direction for the first version: a person signs in on any device and finds their gateway the way they left it. Conversations, tasks, routines, bots, memory, skills and files already live on the gateway, so the account's job there is only that every device reaches the same gateway. The rest is the app's own state about the person, surveyed here from the code.

**The rule for what may follow through the gateway.** The store is `users.prefs` on the gateway's owner profile, which anything with `operator.write` can change on 2026.10.6, and anything with `operator.read` on 2026.11.10: a device let in by a setup code, even a read-only one, or a plugin. No tool a bot holds can call it (§3). So only what can't hide anything or widen anything follows the person that way. A switch that hides what bots do follows **only in the direction that shows more**: turning it on on one device turns it on everywhere; turning it off stays on that device. Nothing that needs admin to change, and no limit on a run, is driven from that store.

**Where what follows is kept: on the gateway** (recommended). It is the one store every device already reaches, it is the person's own, and Clawtrate already writes there: `users.prefs` holds up to 4,096 bytes of JSON per key, read with `operator.read` and written with `operator.write` as a compare-and-set, with a `users.prefs.changed` event (`packages/core/src/sidebar.ts`). One new key, `clawtrate.settings`, holds what follows, versioned and bounded like the sidebar's, and each device reads it under the rule above, so a value turned off there is ignored for the "follows when turned on" switches. Two of a person's devices are the same user while `gateway.roles` is unset (§3); with it set, nothing follows, and each device keeps its own, as the rail already does.

The other way, sealed on the account service with the list secret, would make the settings follow a person across gateways and keep them while a gateway is off, and only the person's own devices could write them. It costs a second store and its conflicts. Not recommended for the first version, which has one gateway per account; it is the place for the switches above if they should ever follow in both directions.

**A brand-new device, the moment after sign-in:** once the join ends and the gateway approves it (seconds), it connects and reads, as any connection does now, the bots, the task list, the rail's arrangement and `clawtrate.settings`. It looks like the person's other devices from its first screen, with each conversation loading when opened. What it doesn't have: drafts, the window's layout, a switch turned off elsewhere, the run limit and "New bots too", which start at their defaults.

## 7. Sign-in

### 7.1 The first version: GitHub and Google

**Shape (proposed).** The account service is the only OAuth client registered with each provider: one GitHub OAuth app and one Google web client, with their redirect addresses on the service's own domain and their secrets on the service. A Clawtrate app signs in to the account service, not to the provider: it opens the system browser at the account service with a one-time PKCE challenge (a hash of a secret the app keeps), the service sends the person to the provider and back, and then returns to the app with a one-time code that only the app's secret redeems.

- **Desktop** (macOS, Windows, Linux): the return is `http://127.0.0.1:<port>/callback` on a port the app opened for that sign-in alone; the system browser, never a window inside the app. This works the same on all three systems.
- **iPhone**: `ASWebAuthenticationSession`, which returns to the app that started it.
- **The session** the service then issues is bound to the device's session key: each request is signed by it, in the session context only (§4.2), so a copied session token is useless elsewhere and a session signature can never be taken for a list entry.
- **What is asked of the provider**: only who the person is. GitHub: no scopes (the public profile). Google: `openid` and `profile`. No repository access, no email reading, nothing else. The provider's token is read once and discarded (§5).

| Provider | Registration                                                                                                   | The account's key for the person                           | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GitHub   | One OAuth app (free). Its web flow needs the client secret, so the exchange happens on the service.            | the numeric user id, never the login, which can be renamed | PKCE (S256) and loopback addresses with any port are supported ([GitHub's docs](https://docs.github.com/en/apps/oauth-apps/building-oauth-apps/authorizing-oauth-apps)). Its tokens don't expire, so each is revoked after use. |
| Google   | One OAuth client of the web type (free); the consent screen's brand verified for the logo; no sensitive scopes | the ID token's `sub`, never the email                      | Desktop clients can't keep a secret ([Google's docs](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/native-app)); the web client on the service avoids that.                                                           |

**The same person with both.** Each sign-in is its own account until linked. Settings → Account → "Also sign in with Google" (while signed in with GitHub, or the reverse) adds the second to the same account. Accounts are never joined by a matching email: an email can change hands or be unverified, and joining by it would let someone who controls one sign-in take over the other's account. A person who signs in with the other provider by mistake sees "No gateway on this account" and the other provider's button beside it.

**More providers.** Sign-ins are rows of (provider, the provider's id for the person) pointing at an account. Another OpenID Connect provider is one registration and one small module.

### 7.2 Later: signing in with ChatGPT

OpenAI documents [Sign in with ChatGPT](https://developers.openai.com/siwc/quickstart) (OpenID Connect with PKCE, a client id from OpenAI). Its quickstart says identity sign-in is "currently available to selected commercial partners through a limited trial", and that what is open to open-source projects is using a ChatGPT plan for AI requests (read 2026-11-25). The second isn't the account's business: model access stays on the gateway. So a third sign-in waits until OpenAI lets Clawtrate register a client for identity; it then fits §7.1 unchanged.

## 8. First-run flows, counted

Clicks count presses in Clawtrate and in the account page; "typed" counts what goes into Clawtrate. Signing in at Google or GitHub, when the browser isn't already, is the provider's and is counted apart.

### 8.1 The same computer, no account

Unchanged and not made worse: "This computer" → **Connect**: 1 click, nothing typed, no approval (#629). The one exception: on a gateway whose owner turned silent local pairing off, that first connection needs one approval outside Clawtrate, once, on OpenClaw's own page or by its approve command (`setup-effortless.md` §12); nothing already paired could approve it. Nothing in this flow mentions an account. After it, Settings → Gateway offers "Use this gateway from your other devices" quietly, once.

### 8.2 Another computer: a gateway on Windows, Clawtrate on a Mac

| Where                                | Steps                                                                                                  | Clicks | Typed |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------ | ----- |
| Gateway's machine, once              | This computer → Connect; Link with Google; Continue with Google; the provider's consent the first time | 3 to 4 | 0     |
| The Mac                              | Sign in; Continue with Google                                                                          | 2      | 0     |
| Gateway's machine (or any device in) | pick the Mac's words                                                                                   | 1      | 0     |

On a gateway whose owner turned silent local pairing off, the Windows PC's first Connect needs one approval outside Clawtrate, once, as §8.1 says; everything after it is as counted. The connector's own pairing (§4.3) and the Mac's pass, first pairing and full control (§4.4 steps 6 to 8) add no click and nothing to read. When the connector sets the gateway's proxy trust during linking, the gateway closes every open connection, so Clawtrate's connection line on the Windows PC blinks once and comes back within a second or so; it is a reconnect, not an error.

Before the review, the Mac reaches the gateway when the two are on the same network or the same private network, through the connector's direct listener (§11). A gateway in a Windows virtual machine is a common case: if that machine sits behind its host's own network address translation, nothing on the home network can reach it until the relay ships, and the Mac says so plainly ("Your gateway's computer isn't reachable from here yet") rather than failing. A virtual machine on a bridged network works before.

The first screen on the Mac gains a third answer to "Where does OpenClaw run?": "I use Clawtrate on another device: Sign in". F-001's "no account to sign in to" stays true: nothing requires it.

### 8.3 A phone (#14, #286)

Sign in (1), Continue with Google (1, and the system's "wants to use google.com to sign in" prompt, 1), pick the words on a device already in (1). Nothing typed. Before the review it reaches the gateway only on the same network or a private network; with the relay, anywhere. The setup code of #14 and the relay code of #286 stay for someone with no account, and #286's design becomes the account-less path rather than the only one: its "Approve from here" is what a member does in §4.4, and its "Remote access" row in Settings is this design's linked gateway.

### 8.4 A gateway on a server with no screen

One command on the server, which installs the connector from Clawtrate's signed release channel as a service of the person's own account (a user service on Linux) and prints a sign-in address and code; the person opens it on any device and signs in there (the provider's device sign-in shape, RFC 8628, offered by the account service). The page that takes the code says, before anything else, "Continuing links a gateway on a server to your account. Continue only if you ran the install command yourself, just now." The first device:

1. On any computer or phone, the person opens Clawtrate and signs in to the same account (2 clicks). It finds the server's gateway on the account with no device on it yet and starts a join.
2. The connector answers that one join itself and prints the five sets of words, numbered, with "n: none of these match" and "x: I didn't start this", in the terminal where the install command ran.
3. The person types the number of the set the device shows, and Enter, once.
4. The connector writes the list's first entry with that device as its first device member, signed by the connector and by the device, carrying the join's transcript hash; seals the first list secret and the card to the device inside its confirmation; and the device verifies them as in §4.4 step 5, then pairs as in §4.4 steps 6 to 8.

The service still can't admit anyone: the confirmation is typed on the gateway's own machine, by the person who just ran the install command there, and the words cover both sides' keys and the card. The connector answers a join only this once, while its list has no device member, and only while the terminal the install command ran in is still open and showing it, at most 30 minutes; after that the person runs the command again; every later device is confirmed on a member, as everywhere else. Typed: the one command, and one number in the same terminal. Apart from the one approval of §8.1 on a gateway whose owner turned silent local pairing off, this is the only flow with a terminal, because there is nowhere else to click.

### 8.5 Unlinking, and a gateway linked to the wrong account

Settings → Gateway → "Linked to <account>" → **Unlink** (on the gateway's machine or with a member's signature): one click and a confirmation, which lists the devices the account added and offers "Also remove these devices from the gateway" ticked. The connector takes its own entries out of `gateway.trustedProxies` (with `replacePaths`) when the setting is still as it wrote it, and otherwise leaves it and says so (§4.3); the gateway closes every open connection once more, and Clawtrate reconnects. Then the connector forgets its keys and removes its own pairing last.

## 9. Hosting and what it costs

### 9.1 Direction and recommendation

Direction: the default path costs nothing to run. A hosted relay is for when the project grows.

**Recommended first version:** the account service on Cloudflare's free plan, in the project's own account; no relay. Devices carry their conversations to the gateway over routes the person already has. The one hosted thing is small: sign-ins, the linked gateway, device rows and a waiting room.

### 9.2 What runs for $0, and what breaks first

Cloudflare's limits as published on 2026-11-25 ([Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/), [Durable Objects pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/)):

| Free plan        | Limit                                                       | Used by                           |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Workers requests | 100,000 a day, 10 ms of CPU each                            | every call to the service         |
| D1 (database)    | 5 million rows read and 100,000 written a day, 5 GB         | accounts, gateways, devices       |
| Durable Objects  | 100,000 requests and 13,000 GB-s a day; SQLite storage only | the waiting room, later the relay |
| Outgoing traffic | not charged                                                 | —                                 |

Over a free limit, further calls of that kind fail until 00:00 UTC.

**The account service.** Assumed per device in daily use: about ten requests a day (refreshing the session, opening the waiting room's socket a few times, reading the account). Joins are rare. So the free plan's 100,000 requests fit about **10,000 devices in daily use**; requests run out first, long before the database. Assumptions to measure once built.

**The relay, after the review.** A connected device's traffic is the gateway's events: a run streams hundreds to thousands of them. Assumed: about 25,000 messages a day for a device in daily use, nearly all from the connector. Durable Objects bill incoming WebSocket messages at 20 to a request and nothing for outgoing ones, and charge no time while a socket hibernates between messages. That is about 1,300 requests a day per device, so the free plan fits about **75 devices in daily use**; at an assumed 2 ms of work per message, time (about 6 GB-s a day per device) runs out much later. Requests run out first here too. The live view of a bot's computer adds far more messages than text and would lower that.

**The person's own machine as the relay.** The connector already runs there, but a machine behind a home router can't be reached from outside without something outside: a port opened on the router (often off, and a risk), or a meeting point that helps the two ends reach each other directly (which the account service could be, cheaply) with a relay still needed when either end is behind carrier-grade address translation, as phones on cellular often are. Not in the first version; worth measuring later as the way to keep most relayed traffic off the project's bill.

**The person's own private network.** Where the gateway's machine is on a Tailscale network or the like, the connector listens there too and the card carries that address, so a device on the same network reaches the gateway from anywhere, for $0 to the project. Clawtrate never sets that network up.

### 9.3 Where it stops being free, and after

| Devices in daily use | Account service            | Relay (after the review)       |
| -------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| 100                  | $0                         | $0 to $5 a month               |
| 10,000               | $0 to $5 a month           | about $90 a month              |
| 1,000,000            | about $100 to $150 a month | about $8,000 to $9,000 a month |

From Cloudflare's paid plan: $5 a month for 10 million Worker requests ($0.30 a million after), 1 million Durable Object requests ($0.15 a million after) and 400,000 GB-s ($12.50 a million after); D1's writes beyond 50 million a month at $1 a million. Every number in this table rests on the assumptions above and is an order of magnitude, not a quote. What drives cost is relayed traffic, and within it, the computer's live view.

### 9.4 What the first version accepts

- **Limits.** Past about 10,000 devices in daily use, the service stops for the rest of each day once its free requests are spent. The $5 plan is the fix.
- **Dependence on one free plan.** If Cloudflare changes it, the service moves to the paid plan or to the self-hosted runner on a small server. Nothing about the person's data moves, because the service holds almost none, and the lists live on the devices and the connector.
- **No reach from anywhere** until the relay, which also waits for the review. When it comes, the free plan carries a small number of people; a share per connector per day keeps one heavy user from spending everyone's, and the paid plan follows use.

### 9.5 Self-hosting

The service is open source, and the same code runs as one Node process with SQLite, as #281's relay draft had a Node runner. Hosting it takes a machine with a public HTTPS address, a GitHub OAuth app and a Google OAuth client registered to that address (free, a few minutes each in their consoles), and the address set once in Clawtrate's Settings → Advanced → Account service. A self-hosted service is its own set of accounts. The relay, later, is the same.

### 9.6 When the hosted service is down

Everything that is connected or can connect keeps working: a gateway on the same computer, a typed address, SSH, and an account device over the connector's direct listener, because a device keeps its card and its gateway token, and the connector keeps its list. Nothing on a connection asks the service. Removing a device still works from Settings while connected to the gateway, because the connector also follows the gateway's own device list (§4.6). What stops: signing in a new device, joining, and the account page. After the relay ships, relayed connections stop while the relay is down; direct ones don't.

## 11. Before and after the cryptographic review

**The direct listener, before the review (proposed).** The connector listens on the gateway machine's local network and private-network addresses (never on a public address) with TLS 1.3, using Node's own TLS with a self-signed certificate whose key is pinned in the card. Each device presents its TLS client key's certificate, and the connector lets in only members. It is the HTTP end, not a pipe:

- **One request per connection.** Each TLS connection carries either one pass request (§4.4 step 6) or exactly one WebSocket upgrade, and is closed after anything else.
- **The upgrade is the connector's own.** It builds the request to the gateway itself from an allowlist of headers (`Host`, `Upgrade`, `Connection`, `Sec-WebSocket-Key`, `Sec-WebSocket-Version`, `Sec-WebSocket-Protocol`, `Sec-WebSocket-Extensions`, `User-Agent`). The header rule, from what ran on both versions (§3):
  - **always** set `X-Forwarded-For` to the device's own socket address: without it the gateway takes the connection as local and pairs it silently;
  - **overwrite** any `X-Forwarded-For` the device sent, never pass it on or append to it: passed on, it lets the device choose the address the gateway records;
  - **drop** `Forwarded` and `X-Real-IP` (the gateway refuses them), every other `X-Forwarded-*` (an identity in trusted-proxy sign-in), `Tailscale-*`, `Cf-Access-*` and `Remote-User`, and every header not on the allowlist.
- **The door.** After the upgrade it carries WebSocket messages itself, and reads the first one the device sends before passing anything on (§4.5).
- **Never loopback.** A connection whose source address is loopback (a tunnel to the listener) is closed; the gateway would refuse it anyway (§3).
- **Proxy trust exactly loopback.** `gateway.trustedProxies` gains exactly `127.0.0.1` and `::1`. Nothing narrower exists: `trustedProxies` takes only addresses and ranges (read from source). The connector refuses to link, saying why, while the gateway's trusted-proxy sign-in allows loopback, because there a forwarded identity header is a credential (ran, both versions). It doesn't refuse on `gateway.nodes.pairing.autoApproveCidrs`, which ignores every address that arrives through a loopback proxy and so never applies to a device the connector forwards; it applies only to devices that reach the gateway directly from a network, which linking doesn't change. What proxy trust still changes: a local process can choose the address the gateway's sign-in rate limit counts against. A local process still needs a credential, and the shared token is long and random, so that costs nothing in practice; it is stated here because it is a change.

This is a standard construction and not the relay protocol, so it doesn't wait for that review. On Windows, a program that listens on the local network brings up the system's firewall prompt the first time (not established on the installer's path; §12).

### The Windows half

Written to be handed over as it stands; Windows work in this repository belongs to its own contributor.

1. **Secure storage on Windows.** Electron's `safeStorage` uses DPAPI there. Without it the app has no full access, so it can hold neither `operator.admin` nor `operator.pairing`, and can neither link nor confirm. First, and needed by #629 too. DPAPI keeps secrets from other users, not from other programs of the same user (§10).
2. **Connecting on the same Windows computer.** Reading OpenClaw's settings on Windows (`%USERPROFILE%\.openclaw\openclaw.json` for a native install) without running OpenClaw's programs, and a send gate that proves the other end of the loopback socket is this account's gateway (the owning process from the TCP table, its user, its image, the profile's lock naming it), the counterpart of `apps/desktop/src/setup/local-prove.ts` (Windows has none yet, as the header of `settings-reader.ts` says). A gateway inside WSL (OpenClaw's Windows app keeps one of its own, `<openclaw>/docs/platforms/windows.md` in 2026.11.10) answers loopback through WSL's own relay, so this proof can't name it, and it needs its own design; until then the connector for such a gateway runs inside WSL as on a server (§8.4).
3. **The connector's lifetime on Windows.** Running while Clawtrate runs, and an option to start at sign-in to Windows (a per-user Scheduled Task or the Run key) so devices reach the gateway when the app is closed. No service, no administrator rights. The pass to the connector goes on its standard input, as on macOS.
4. **The firewall.** The direct listener on the local network: whether the installer adds a rule for the connector (it already runs with the rights to) or the system prompts on first listen, and what the person sees either way. Not established (§14).
5. **The sign-in return.** The system browser opening and returning to `http://127.0.0.1:<port>/callback` on Windows, through the default browser, with the loopback port chosen by the app.
6. **Checks.** The connector's pairing, the join, the pass and the connector's approvals against a development gateway on Windows, as the macOS checks will run them.

## 14. Not established, and what would establish it

**Established by the spike (A3): ran, both versions,** on development gateways only, never a person's gateway:

1. `device.pair.setupCode` with `publicUrl` set to the gateway's own loopback `ws://` address returns a code on a gateway bound to loopback, with the default ("full") profile; without `publicUrl` it refuses; the token decodes from the code.
2. A client presenting that pass on a direct loopback socket, as `gateway-client`/`ui`, role `operator`, asking for the six, is paired silently with the six; with `autoApproveLocal: false` it waits, and a full-control device's `device.pair.approve` lets it in on its next hello with the same pass, with the six.
3. A reconnect on a device token asking for more than the token holds is refused (`AUTH_SCOPE_MISMATCH`) and files nothing. A device paired with a default pass holds the six already; one paired with a limited pass upgrades only through `device.scopes.requestUpgrade`.
4. Through a forwarder that sets `X-Forwarded-For`, with `trustedProxies` at exactly loopback, a new device presenting its own pass files a pending request with the forwarder's address; after one approval its next hello gets a device token, and it then holds the six. It can read and send.
5. A pass presented by a second key is refused, before and after the first key's approval; a pass older than 10 minutes is refused; two passes are independent. A pass isn't used up by a Clawtrate client's pairing and can't be revoked.
6. `device.pair.list` shows, for each pending request, its request id, device id, public key, roles, scopes, client id and mode, and its remote address when the connection isn't local.
7. A forwarded connection with no `X-Forwarded-For` is local and pairs silently; with the device's own value, remote at the device's chosen address; with a loopback value, or `Forwarded`, `X-Real-IP` or `X-Forwarded-User` alone, refused.
8. Changing `trustedProxies` doesn't restart either version, and closes every open connection within a few seconds; they reconnect at once.
9. A pass presented as OpenClaw's own iOS app in node mode, through the forwarder, is paired with no approval and given an operator token with the profile's scopes.
10. `autoApproveCidrs` has no effect on a forwarded device; trusted-proxy sign-in with `allowLoopback` lets a forwarded identity header stand in for a credential.
11. On 2026.11.10 a read-only device can call `users.prefs.set`; on 2026.10.6 it needs write.
